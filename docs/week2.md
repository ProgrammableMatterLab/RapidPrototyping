## Hardware & Software


### Hardware

#### Laser cutters
**Laser cutter model**: The Mill houses 2x Laser cutters, [ULS Ultra R9000](https://app.supademo.com/demo/cmmxufkij00hath0i7rym83nu?utm_source=link). One is configured as a CO<sub>2</sub> laser, the other is a dual CO<sub>2</sub> and Fiber laser. In general, CO<sub>2</sub> lasers excel at cutting and engraving organic, non-metal materials like wood and acrylic, while Fiber lasers are optimized for cutting metals. We will be using plywood, using the CO<sub>2</sub> laser, so either laser can be used.

**Reservations**: Both laser cutters are reserved for us during our lab times. Other classes may reserve them for their own class times; all other times are first-come first-served. You can call the Mill to check their availability or check the [Laser cutter schedule](https://reg.hfs.uw.edu/Facility/GetSchedule?facilityId=f5ee6377-94cc-4c95-8611-a42f9d3e62af) on the [Mill homepage](https://reg.hfs.uw.edu/).

**USBs**: you'll need a USB to transfer your Illustrator files from the Mill computers to the Laser Cutter. The Mill always tries to keep a large stash of USBs to use, but it might be useful to bring your own as a backup. 

#### Calipers
Calipers will be useful for checking dimensions of your cut plywood sheets. These are available from the Mill front desk. See the [Calipers overview](https://makezine.com/article/digital-fabrication/3d-printing-workshop/how-to-use-your-digital-calipers-7-tips/) from the Readings.

#### Mallet
A rubber mallet can optionally be used to gently tap tight laser-cut finger joints into place without damaging the material. This is available in the Mill.

#### Materials
We will use Basswood plywood, 12" x 12" squares of 2.7mm thickness (thicknes may vary slightly). Course materials will be stored in the Mill Storage Room. However, you are also welcome to use any of the _scrap material_ on the shelf between the 2 laser cutters that has been left behind by others.

### Design and laser cut finger joint parts

You will begin work on your **assignment** task to produce a laser-cut computer mouse enclosure. Start during the lab, and finish on your own time. You should have **completed all the readings** when you arrive in lab.

**You will need to submit the assignment individually, but feel free to work in pairs during lab.**

#### What to do in the lab
First, read the Assignment **Task** description below. Then proceed to read this section.

To succeed on the assignment task, your goal during the lab should be to design and laser-cut at least two mating finger-joint parts. These could even be two simple squares connected by a single finger joint. This will let you go through the process of design and fabrication on the laser cutter:
1. Design mating finger joints in Onshape. Dimension or offset to account for kerf (enlarge sketches by 0.25mm on all sides).
1. Export your file as a DXF and save to a USB (the Mill has spare USBs).
1. Use a Mill computer to access Illustrator. Place your parts on the canvas true-to-size. Ensure the document is set to RGB color, and set your part outline to red (R=255,G=0,B=0) and line width to 0.25pt.
1. Save as an .ai file to USB, and transfer to laser cutter for cutting.
1. Try to mate your cut parts. You can borrow Calipers from the Mill to check dimensions.

### Task: Laser-cut Mouse Enclosure

#### Task learning objectives
The goals of this assignment are to:
1. Learn to produce 2D drawings on Onshape and adjust for laser cutting kerf.
1. Use illustrator to process Onshape drawings for laser cutting.
1. Laser cut mating parts.

#### Task Deliverables: Low fidelity laser-cut mouse

There are 3 deliverables for the laser-cut mouse: CAD documentation, fabrication documentation, and final physical prototype. 

At a minimum, you should complete:
1. CAD screenshots showing the development of your design, with dimensioning.
1. Photos taken of your laser-cut parts
1. A photo of your physical mouse. It should have an appropriate size and shape (e.g. box-shaped), be laser cut with joints (e.g. finger joints) to allow friction-fit mating of parts, and feature a rastering (ideally to visualize features difficult to cut, e.g. buttons). Note that the **laser-cut box tutorial** reading is highly relevant. 

For full credit, you can try some of the following:
1. Make a mouse that is more complex than a simple (box-shaped) rectangular prism by joining several box-like structures.
1. Try an entirely different architecture using stacked slices or slotted slices; see inspiration on this [U Greenwich blog](https://blogs.gre.ac.uk/architecture/2013/08/21/laser-cut-3d-sliced-models/).
1. Raster additional visual features on to your mouse.
1. Make cuts and partition your laser-cut panels to allow the mouse PCB to be easily inserted, and for the USB cable to exit the enclosure.
1. Incorporate a living hinge into your mouse to allow partial assembly by folding. See this [Brief Ponoko overview](https://www.ponoko.com/blog/how-to-make/how-to-design-a-living-hinge/) and these [Instructables examples](https://www.instructables.com/Curved-laser-bent-wood/) 
1. Instead of a mouse, incorporate some of these "advanced" features into an entirely different model of your choosing.

In your documentation, you should  discuss the development of your work; your design decisions, things you tried that did or didn't work, helpful resources you used, suprising outcomes etc.

To submit your assignment, document it under a Week 2 heading on your Google Site, then publish it.
